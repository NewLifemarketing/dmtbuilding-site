The City counts your addition before anyone draws it

Most home additions Toronto homeowners plan are judged by three numbers long before the first drawing: the floor area being added, the distance from the new footing to the nearest protected tree, and the number of dwelling units the house will hold when it is done. The City of Toronto attaches different paperwork, and sometimes a different bill, to each of them.

None of that shows up on a mood board. It shows up at the permit counter, or partway through excavation. This page covers the three, in the order they bite, and where all three end up: the foundation.

Measurement one: is it 100 square metres?

Toronto has a line drawn at 100 square metres, and it is not in the zoning by-law. It is in Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 519, "Infill Construction, Public Notice", in force since September 4, 2018 as part of the City's Residential Infill Strategy. Under it, an addition or additions to an existing house with a combined total area of 100 square metres or more needs a public notice posted on the site.

That notice is not a formality you print yourself. You send Toronto Building the project details through the Infill Public Notice Declaration form, including the owner and builder contacts, any Committee of Adjustment decision, and front and rear elevations as black-and-white line drawings. Toronto Building returns a print-ready file. The City's rules then set the rest:

  • Printed at 24 by 18 inches on Coroplast or corrugated polypropylene, with no changes to the content.
  • Posted at the front of the site, facing the street, within five business days of construction starting.
  • A photo emailed to Toronto Building Inspections within 24 hours of putting it up.
  • Kept up until an occupancy permit is issued.

Below the line, none of this applies. Above it, your Toronto neighbours get your elevations and your builder's name on a sign by the sidewalk. Know which side of 100 square metres you are on before the design settles.

Measurement two: how close is the nearest protected tree?

In a Toronto backyard this is the one that most often moves an addition. The City of Toronto's Tree Protection By-law protects any tree on private property 30 centimetres or more in diameter, and every tree on a City street regardless of size. Diameter is measured 1.4 metres above the ground. A boundary tree, one whose trunk crosses a property line, belongs to both owners, and the City needs the neighbour's consent on any permit for it.

What matters for an addition is not the trunk but the Tree Protection Zone around it. The City publishes minimum zone sizes by diameter, and says that if any construction work, including access or storage, will reach into that zone, you need a permit to injure or remove the tree. Excavating for a footing is on the City's own list of activities that injure trees. So is driving equipment over roots, and so is storing material in the zone.

Two dates make this more pressing now than a year ago. On September 1, 2026 the City introduced a Distinctive Tree category for large, healthy trees on private property and in ravines more than 61 centimetres in diameter, which City staff identify and which must be considered through the permit. And City Council has endorsed lowering the private tree threshold from 30 to 20 centimetres, pending a report to committee in 2027. A tree that does not count today may count by the time a slow project breaks ground.

Toronto recommends an arborist measure the zone before you design. Put the zone on the site plan, then draw the addition. A footprint that steps around a root zone costs a few lines on a drawing. One that runs through it costs a permit, compensation planting, and possibly the tree.

If the yard backs onto a ravine

Toronto's ravines carry their own by-law, Chapter 658, the Ravine and Natural Feature Protection By-law. Inside a mapped protection area the City prohibits, without a permit, harming any tree regardless of size, changing the land's grade by excavating or adding soil on slopes, placing fill, and building new structures or retaining walls. You can check a property on the City's online map by switching on the "Ravine and Natural Feature Protection By-law" layer. From September 1, 2026, ravine permits carry application fees: $262.72 per tree on a construction permit, and $632.51 for a grade alteration with no trees involved.

The Toronto and Region Conservation Authority (TRCA) may be involved too. TRCA treats a home addition as development activity under Ontario Regulation 41/24, and where a property falls within its regulated area, the City cannot legally issue the building permit until TRCA has issued its own permit or a clearance. TRCA asks for a lot survey, a grading plan showing the top of slope, and elevations giving geodetic heights of the lowest openings; it may also ask for a geotechnical slope stability assessment. For a Toronto property, TRCA asks that any tree injury or removal the work causes be submitted to the City's ravine protection staff at the same time, not after.

TRCA's list also asks for the setback from the top of slope to the new work, and for erosion and sediment control fencing to keep construction debris out of the ravine and the sewer. Both are drawn on paper and then built by whoever digs the footing, which on our jobs is our own crew.

Measurement three: one home, or more than one?

The third number is the unit count, and it is the one that decides whether an addition owes development charges. The City of Toronto's own overview lists the enlargement of an existing dwelling unit among the exemptions set by provincial legislation. A larger kitchen, a second storey, a primary bedroom over the garage: if the house stays one home, the addition is enlarging it.

An addition that increases the number of residential units is a different case. The City lists it among the projects that may be subject to charges, and then lists exemptions for creating additional units, as prescribed by the province. Whether your second unit is exempt is a question for Toronto Building at the start. Toronto's zoning also now allows garden suites in most residential zones and its permit guides cover multiplex conversions up to six units, so the choice between building on, building behind, and converting is a real one here.

What the City adds to home addition cost Toronto owners pay

Nobody can price your addition from a web page. But part of the home addition cost Toronto owners face is set by the City, and it is published. The Small Residential Additions guide lists a 2026 service index of $18.56 per square metre of proposed work for single family dwellings, semis and townhouses, and $56.33 for each new residential unit in an application. On a 40 square metre addition, that index works out to about $742 before anything else on the City's fee page applies.

Then there are the items that are easy to leave out of a budget:

  • A Municipal Road Damage deposit, held against damage to the curb, sidewalk, road, laneway or boulevard, and against mud on the street or uncleared snow while work is underway.
  • Tree permit fees and compensation planting if the design reaches into a protected tree's zone, and ravine permit fees on a ravine lot.
  • A TRCA permit fee where a Toronto property sits in TRCA's regulated area.
  • Committee of Adjustment costs if the addition needs a minor variance from Zoning By-law 569-2013.

For the structure itself, we quote every job individually and itemize it. If you want to understand where an ICF premium comes from before you call, our post on what ICF actually costs lays it out plainly.

Zoning, and the old by-laws underneath it

Toronto's Zoning By-law 569-2013 is city-wide because each municipality that amalgamated into Toronto in 1998 brought its own by-law. Most Toronto properties now fall under 569-2013, but some are still affected by a former municipal by-law, and those show in grey on the City of Toronto zoning map. Know which by-law you are reading before a designer works out what your lot allows.

If the addition does not fit, the route is a minor variance at Toronto's Committee of Adjustment. Since November 28, 2022, only the applicant, the Minister, specified persons and public bodies can appeal its decision.

The permit application then asks foundation questions dressed as paperwork: footing and foundation wall details, the height of exterior grade above the basement floor, and grade elevations to confirm height. Toronto Building takes these online, and its submission tool shows which outside approvals apply.

Home Additions Toronto — FAQs

Does my addition need a public notice sign in Toronto?

Only if the combined area of the additions is 100 square metres or more. Under Toronto Municipal Code Chapter 519, that notice is prepared by Toronto Building, posted facing the street within five business days of construction starting, and kept up until an occupancy permit is issued.

There is a big tree near where we want to build. What now?

Have an arborist measure it. Private trees 30 centimetres or more in diameter are protected, and if construction, access or storage will reach into the tree's protection zone, the City requires a permit application to injure or remove it.

Will a Toronto addition owe development charges?

Not if it only enlarges your existing home; the City lists that as exempt. An addition that adds dwelling units may be subject to charges, with exemptions for additional units as prescribed by the province. Confirm with Toronto Building.

Our lot backs onto a ravine. Is that a problem?

It adds steps. The City's ravine by-law requires a permit for grade changes, new structures and harm to any tree in a protected area, and TRCA may require its own permit before the City can issue the building permit.

Are you based in Toronto?

No. We are based in Barrie, and Toronto is on the list of Greater Toronto Area cities we publish as our service area.

Can an ICF addition join a brick or framed Toronto house?

Yes. The connection is engineered for the specific house, and we build it to those details. Brick, stone or siding can go over the new walls to match.
