The size of the room is the third question in Markham, not the first

Most people planning home additions Markham-wide start with square footage: how big, how many rooms, how much. The City of Markham asks something else first. Its building permit process page lists additions to existing buildings among the work that always needs a permit, with no minimum size. What changes from one Markham house to the next is not whether you need a permit. It is how many other approvals have to be in hand before you are even allowed to apply.

Two facts about your property decide that. The first is whether the house sits inside one of Markham's four heritage conservation districts, or is individually designated. The second is how much of the existing ground surface the addition will disturb. Get those two answers early and the rest of the job has a schedule. Find them out at the permit counter and it has a delay.

We are DMT Building Group. We are based in Barrie, Ontario, and we build across the Greater Toronto Area. Markham is not one of the towns we list by name on our service area, so we will be straight about that: we are not a Markham company, and you will not find a local office here. What we bring to a home addition Markham owners are planning is the structural half of it. We build additions in insulated concrete form (ICF), and our own crews do the footings, the foundation, the walls and the waterproofing.

Question one: is the house in a heritage district?

The City of Markham has protected four areas as heritage conservation districts: Thornhill, Markham Village, Unionville and Buttonville. On top of those, the City says more than 350 properties are individually designated, and it keeps a searchable Register of Property of Cultural Heritage Value or Interest so you can check your own address.

If your house is in one of the four districts, or on that register as designated, the City's heritage permit page is explicit about two things. A residential addition needs a Major Heritage Permit, not a minor one. And a building permit application cannot be submitted until the heritage permit is obtained. The heritage review is not a step that runs beside the building permit. It runs before it.

The same page describes what happens to a Major application. It is screened for completeness, circulated to staff, and in most cases reviewed by the Heritage Markham Committee, which makes a recommendation. Applications that comply with existing policy can be approved by staff. Only Markham Council can refuse one. Each district also has its own plan, approved by Council, with guidelines for how new work should sit beside the old, and that plan is what your designer draws the addition against.

Note the dashed lines. A heritage approval does not let you skip the grading question; it only has to come first. The City's RGS page says an RGS application is not taken while a Heritage Permit application is still waiting on a decision, so on a Markham Village or Unionville house the two run one after the other, never side by side.

Question two: how much ground does the addition touch?

Outside the heritage districts, the line that matters is 50 square metres. Markham's Residential Infill Grading and Servicing process, run by the City's Engineering department, applies to a residential addition with an impact of 50 m² or more to the existing ground surface. The City calls that a Type II application. An addition that disturbs less than 50 m², or that makes no ground-level alteration at all, is outside it.

Read the wording closely, because it is about the ground and not the room. A second storey over an existing footprint may never touch the yard. A one-storey family room off the back can disturb far more ground than its floor area once you count the dig for the foundation, the working room around it, a new walkout and the regrading to drain it all away from the house. Your designer should measure the disturbed area on the survey before anyone assumes which side of the line you are on.

If you are over it, the RGS application goes in through ePLAN with a grading and servicing plan and a signed RGS requirements checklist. Depending on the lot, the City may also want a topographic survey, a tree report, utility locates, a stormwater management brief, a CCTV inspection of the existing sewer connection, any heritage permits, and TRCA clearance. The City allows up to 30 days for each review cycle and asks applicants to resolve its comments within three cycles. When the plan is accepted, you receive a stamped copy, and only then may you apply for the building permit.

  • TRCA. The Toronto and Region Conservation Authority regulates development in or near valleys, streams and wetlands under Ontario Regulation 41/24, and the Regional Municipality of York is one of its participating municipalities. TRCA's Regulated Area Search shows whether your lot is in a regulated area.
  • Trees. Markham's Tree Preservation By-law 2023-164 protects private trees 20 cm in diameter at breast height or larger. Injuring one needs a permit too, so a protected tree near the new footprint is a question for the design stage, not for the excavation.
  • Zoning. The City's permit page links its Comprehensive Zoning By-law 2024-19 and a Zoning Preliminary Review form. The site plan in your permit drawings has to carry a zoning summary, so setbacks and coverage are checked before a footing is laid out.

Then the building permit, and the clocks that run on it

Markham takes building permit applications through ePLAN only; paper is no longer accepted. There is a $100 non-refundable pre-application fee, then a pre-screen, then an electronic plans review. For houses and small projects, the City's target for a first review is 10 business days when the application is complete and 20 when it is not. Markham names the three things that most often slow an application down: incomplete drawings, zoning compliance mistakes, and incomplete applicable-law approvals. The heritage permit and the RGS acceptance are applicable-law approvals, which is why they sit ahead of this step.

The City's guide to building permits asks a few things of an addition that it does not ask of a new house. Existing floor plans are required, because the reviewer needs to see what the new work connects to. Lot grading approval is listed for additions as well as new homes. And the structural drawings must include the foundation and footing design, which is where an ICF wall shows up on paper.

One cost is easier than people expect. Markham's development charges pamphlet, effective July 1, 2026, lists the enlargement of an existing dwelling unit, or the addition of one dwelling unit within an existing residence, among the exemptions under the City's by-laws and the Development Charges Act. A Markham home addition that makes your own house bigger is generally not the kind of project those charges are aimed at. Confirm your case with the City's Development Finance department before you budget.

    Check the address

    Is the house in Thornhill, Markham Village, Unionville or Buttonville, or on the City's register as designated? That one lookup decides whether the heritage permit leads.

  2. 2

    Measure the ground

    With your designer, mark the excavation and regrading on the survey and see whether the disturbed area reaches 50 m².

  3. 3

    Clear the approvals

    Heritage permit if needed, then RGS if needed, then the building permit through ePLAN with existing plans and the foundation design attached.

  4. 4

    Build and tie in

    Our crews form and pour the footings and raise the ICF walls to the stamped grades, then the opening into the house is made once the new shell is closed in.

Two things in Markham make that third step slower if they are missed: a tree over 20 cm near the dig, and a lot that turns out to be TRCA-regulated. Both are cheap to check before design and expensive to discover after. Home Additions Markham — FAQs

Does every addition in Markham need a building permit?

Yes. The City of Markham lists additions to existing buildings among the work that requires a building permit, with no minimum size. Applications go through ePLAN only.

My house is in Unionville. What changes?

Unionville is one of Markham's four heritage conservation districts, with Thornhill, Markham Village and Buttonville. A residential addition there needs a Major Heritage Permit, and the City says a building permit cannot be submitted until the heritage permit is obtained.

What is an RGS application, and does my addition need one?

Residential Infill Grading and Servicing is the City's review of grading and servicing on infill lots. A residential addition that impacts 50 m² or more of the existing ground surface needs a Type II RGS application, accepted before you apply for the building permit.

Do I pay development charges on an addition?

Generally not for enlarging your own home. Markham's July 1, 2026 development charges pamphlet lists the enlargement of an existing dwelling unit, or adding one unit within an existing residence, among the exemptions. Confirm with Development Finance.

There is a big tree near where the addition will go. Is that a problem?

It can be. Markham's Tree Preservation By-law 2023-164 protects private trees 20 cm in diameter at breast height or larger, and injuring one needs a permit. Plan the footprint around it early.

Are you a Markham company?

No. DMT Building Group is based in Barrie and builds across the Greater Toronto Area. Our own crews build the footings, foundation and ICF walls of the addition.
