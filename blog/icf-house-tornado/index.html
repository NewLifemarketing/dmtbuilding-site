ICF House Tornado

Concrete Walls That Stand Up to Storms

A reinforced concrete core stops the debris that goes straight through a framed wall. The roof stays on only if it is tied to that core.

Most of what gets written about ICF house tornado resistance is about the walls, and the walls are only half the story. The other half is the roof, and how it is fastened to those walls.

That is not a theory. When tornadoes went through Angus in 2014 and south Barrie in 2021, the engineers who surveyed the damage kept finding the same weak point: the nails holding the roof to the walls. A concrete wall does nothing for a roof that is held on with three nails.

What a Tornado Actually Does to a House

A tornado attacks a house two ways at once.

The first is pressure. Wind going over a roof pulls up on it, the way air over a wing lifts a plane. Wind getting inside, through a broken window or a garage door that gave way, pushes up from underneath at the same time. The roof is being pulled and pushed off in the same moment, and the only thing resisting is the connection between the roof framing and the top of the wall.

The second is debris. Everything the wind picks up becomes a projectile: branches, shingles, fence boards, pieces of somebody else's house. A 2x4 travelling end-first at highway speed is a battering ram, and the question for a wall is whether it stops one or lets it through.

Canada rates tornadoes on the Enhanced Fujita scale, which Environment Canada adopted on April 1, 2013. Ratings come from the damage left behind, and each step has a wind speed attached:

  • EF0: 90 to 130 km/h
  • EF1: 135 to 175 km/h
  • EF2: 180 to 220 km/h
  • EF3 and up: 225 km/h and beyond, to EF5 at 315 km/h or more

Researchers at Western University found that more than 95 per cent of Canadian tornadoes from 2017 to 2019 were EF2 or weaker. That is the range worth designing a house around, and it is a range where good building decisions change the outcome.

Two Simcoe County Tornadoes, One Lesson

Simcoe County does not need a hypothetical. It has two recent, well-documented examples.

Angus, June 17, 2014. Environment Canada rated it an EF2 at the top of its class, with peak winds between 200 and 220 km/h. It damaged up to 102 homes, 14 of them beyond repair, left 300 people homeless, and insurance claims passed $30 million.

South Barrie, July 15, 2021. Environment Canada and the Northern Tornadoes Project rated it an EF2, with maximum winds of 210 km/h. City officials put the damage at about 150 homes, many of them unsafe to enter.

Gregory Kopp, a Western University wind engineer, surveyed the Angus damage with his team. The tornado tore the roofs off 11 houses, and the toe-nailed joints holding roofs to walls were, in his words, mostly clearly faulty, often with a single nail where the code asks for three. In Barrie seven years later, the Northern Tornadoes Project found several homes that did not meet the building code for the same reason: toe nails that were missing.

Both were EF2 tornadoes. Neither was the kind of storm nothing can survive. That is what makes them useful to a builder: they are the storms where construction decides the result.

ICF House Tornado Debris: What a 2x4 at 100 MPH Did

The best-known test on this comes from the Wind Engineering Research Center at Texas Tech University, written up by the Portland Cement Association. They built ten wall specimens, framed and concrete, and fired a 2x4 stud at them end-first from a compressed-air cannon at up to 100 mph. The authors picked that weight and speed to match the debris in 99 per cent of tornadoes recorded in the United States.

The results were not close.

  • The framed walls did not stop the stud. In every case it went completely through the wall with little or no damage to the 2x4 itself.
  • The concrete walls stopped it every time. The exterior finishes were damaged, and the concrete behind them was unscathed.
  • The thinnest ICF section tested, a 2-inch web in an older waffle-grid form, was undamaged by a direct hit at over 100 mph.

That last point is worth understanding. A waffle-grid form leaves a wall that is thick in places and thin in others, and even its thin spots held. Nudura, the system we build with, is a flat-wall form: its evaluation report classifies it under ASTM E2634, the standard for ICF walls with a uniform concrete core, so there are no thin spots. Its cores start at 102 mm, roughly twice the section that passed this test.

For an ICF house tornado debris is the hazard people picture first, and it is the one the walls handle best. The foam and the siding on the outside will take a beating. The concrete inside stops what a stud wall lets into the living room.

Why the Core Stops What a Stud Wall Lets Through

A framed wall resists debris with what it has: a sheet of siding, a sheet of sheathing, a stud cavity full of insulation and a sheet of drywall. Every layer is thin, and the cavity in the middle is mostly air. A heavy object travelling fast finds the gaps between studs and goes through.

An ICF wall puts a continuous slab of reinforced concrete across the whole face. There is no cavity to find. Mass absorbs the hit, and the steel holds the concrete together so a strike that cracks the face does not break the wall apart.

The steel is the part people forget. In the forms we use, plastic webs every eight inches hold the reinforcing in position before the pour, so the bars end up where the engineer drew them rather than wherever they settled. That is a big part of why our new builds page says a reinforced concrete core stands up to wind, weather and time in a way conventional framing can't match.

Windows and doors are still openings

A concrete wall with a hole in it is a hole. Glass breaks, garage doors buckle, and an opening that fails lets wind inside, which is the pressure that helps push a roof off from below. The wall around the opening will hold. What fills it decides whether the house stays closed. If wind is a priority on your build, impact-rated glazing and a garage door rated for wind belong in the same conversation as the walls.

The Weak Point Is Where the Roof Meets the Wall

Here is the part an ICF brochure skips.

The National Building Code calls for three nails, just over three inches long, at each spot where a roof truss or rafter sits on a wall. They are driven at an angle, which is what toe-nailing means. Kopp describes those toe-nailed joints as the weak links in the structure, and his group's modelling backs him up. In a 2022 study of a wood roof built to the 2015 code, the first thing to fail was the roof-to-wall connection, at roughly 14 per cent of the load an EF2 tornado would put on it.

That number is the whole argument. A house built with ICF walls and a standard wood roof is a house where the walls can take far more than the roof can. If the roof leaves, the walls are standing with nothing on top of them, and so is everything inside.

ICF house tornado anchoring: tie the roof to the concrete

The good news is that ICF gives the roof something better to hold on to than a wood stud wall does. The top of the wall is concrete. The plate the trusses sit on is anchored into that concrete when it is poured, and from there the chain to the roof is short.

  • Anchor the top plate into the core, at the spacing the engineer sets, so the plate cannot lift off the wall.
  • Strap every truss to that plate with a metal hurricane tie instead of relying on toe nails alone.
  • Treat the whole path as one chain. Roof to plate, plate to concrete, concrete through the steel to the footing. A load goes to the weakest link, and every link has to be designed, not assumed.

Straps are cheap. Kopp puts hurricane straps at less than $200 per house to install. On a house where the walls are already reinforced concrete, they are the least expensive part of making the whole house match its walls.

What an ICF House Will Not Do in a Tornado

It is worth being plain about the limits.

It is not a storm shelter. A purpose-built safe room is an engineered room with a rated roof, a rated door and anchoring designed for a specific wind speed. An ICF house has excellent walls. Unless the roof, floors and openings are designed to the same standard, it is not a shelter, and nobody should treat it as one.

It will not shrug off the worst storms. An EF4 or EF5 is a different event, and a direct hit from one is beyond what any ordinary house is built for, concrete or not.

The house code was not written for tornadoes. Canada's code for houses designs for normal wind loads. CSA published S520:22 in 2022, a standard for building low-rise houses and small buildings to resist high wind, written to supplement the house part of the code. Using it is a choice an owner and a designer make, not a default.

None of that is an argument against ICF. It is an argument for finishing the job: concrete walls do their part on day one, and the rest of the house has to be built to keep up.

Is an ICF House Tornado-Proof?

No house is tornado-proof, and an ICF house tornado claim that says otherwise is selling you a word. What ICF gives you is a set of walls that stop the debris that goes straight through a framed wall, with a reinforced core that has no cavity to punch through.

Whether the whole house holds in an EF1 or EF2, the storms that do most of the damage in this country, comes down to the roof connection and the openings. Get the straps on, tie the plate into the concrete, think about the glazing and the garage door, and the house starts to match its walls.

FAQ

Can an ICF house survive a tornado?

In an ICF house tornado damage usually starts at the roof, not the walls. The walls hold up far better than framed walls, and in testing a reinforced concrete wall stopped a 2x4 fired at up to 100 mph that went straight through framed walls. Whether the whole house survives depends on how the roof is tied to those walls and whether the windows and doors hold. In an EF4 or EF5 direct hit, no ordinary house is built to survive intact.

Will flying debris go through an ICF wall?

In the Texas Tech tests reported by the Portland Cement Association, it did not. Concrete walls stopped a 2x4 fired end-first at up to 100 mph, and even a 2-inch section of an older waffle-grid ICF form was undamaged. The siding and the outer foam can be damaged; the concrete core stops the object.

Do ICF homes need hurricane straps?

Yes, if you want the roof to stay on in a strong wind. The building code's standard roof-to-wall connection is three toe-nails, and Western University research found that connection fails well below the load of an EF2 tornado. Is an ICF house tornado-proof?

No house is. An ICF house has walls that resist wind and debris far better than framing, but the roof, the floors and the openings are usually built the conventional way. It is not a storm shelter unless those parts are engineered to the same standard.

Do tornadoes happen in Simcoe County?

Yes. Environment Canada rated the Angus tornado of June 17, 2014 a top-end EF2 that damaged up to 102 homes, and the south Barrie tornado of July 15, 2021 an EF2 with maximum winds of 210 km/h that damaged about 150 homes. In Angus, the tornado tore the roofs off 11 houses.
