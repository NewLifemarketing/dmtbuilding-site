Plumbing in ICF Walls

Most of the Pipe Never Touches the Wall

Supply lines stay in the partitions, drains leave through sleeves set before the pour, and the few runs that have to go in the foam follow rules of their own.

Plumbing in ICF walls follows one rule before any other: keep the pipe out of the exterior wall wherever you can. That is not an ICF quirk. It is the same rule a good plumber follows in a framed house, and Nudura's own manual says so in its first paragraph on plumbing. What changes with ICF is the handful of places a pipe genuinely has to meet the wall, and every one of them is cheaper to plan before the pour than to fix after it.

Why the Pipes Stay in the Partitions

In any house, hot and cold supply lines belong in interior partition walls and in the floor space under cabinets, tubs and sinks. Vent stacks too, where the layout allows. An exterior wall is the worst place in the building for water: it is the one surface with winter on the other side of it.

Plumbing in ICF walls makes that rule easier to keep and more worth keeping. Easier, because nothing about the wall pulls a pipe into it. There are no stud bays sitting empty and inviting a supply line. More worth keeping, because the inside layer of foam is half the wall's insulation. Every inch of it you cut away for a pipe is an inch that is no longer between the pipe and the concrete.

Nudura puts both reasons in one sentence. Running supply lines and vents through interior partitions leaves the foam on the exterior walls whole and reduces the risk of freezing in a cold climate. The plumbing code applies to the supply, the vents and the drains the same as it does anywhere else.

Plumbing in ICF Walls Starts With the Sleeves

Keeping pipes out of the wall does not mean nothing crosses it. The water service comes in. The main drain goes out, unless it leaves below the footing. Hose bibs, a hot water tank vent, the dryer vent, the HRV ducts and a gas line all need a way through.

Before the concrete goes in, each of those gets a sleeve. The crew cuts a hole through both layers of foam with a keyhole or pruning saw and slides in a length of pipe sized for the service. A few details decide whether it works:

  • Size and location come from the trade. The plumber tells us how big each sleeve is and where it goes. For a drain, that includes the elevation and the slope, because a sleeve set level on a pipe that needs fall is a sleeve in the wrong place.
  • The sleeve dodges the webs. If a sleeve lands on one of the plastic webs that tie the two foam panels together, it moves to one side rather than cutting the web. A cut web needs extra bracing around the opening.
  • Big openings need steel. Anything larger than 16 by 16 inches gets added reinforcing around it.
  • The pipe sticks out both sides. The sleeve runs past the foam face inside and out, so the plumber can put a coupler on it later without digging the foam away to reach it.

Nudura's pre-pour checklist asks one blunt question: have all service penetration sleeves been installed? It is on the list because the answer cannot be changed afterwards without a core drill. That is why we form service penetrations in rather than cut them in later, and why we ask for the plumbing layout at the quote rather than at the pour.

The Main Drain: Plumbing in ICF Walls Below Grade

The main toilet drain and vent stack are usually 4-inch PVC or ABS. If the drain leaves the house under the footing, the wall never sees it. If it does not, it passes through the wall at whatever height lets the waste run by gravity to the septic tank and bed or to the sewer line. That height is the plumber's number, and it has to be right before the sleeve is set.

The outside of that penetration needs care. It is a round hole through a below-grade wall, and surface water will find it. The detail Nudura gives is an EPS-compatible primer or caulking around the pipe where it leaves the foam, before the peel-and-stick membrane goes on over it. Our own crews do the waterproofing, so that detail is ours to get right, and it is covered in more depth in do ICF foundations need waterproofing.

When a Supply Line Has to Run in the Wall

Sometimes the plan wins. A kitchen sink under a window on an exterior wall needs water, and the partition is on the other side of the room. A supply line in the foam is allowed. It is the one part of plumbing in ICF walls that has to be done the way the wall wants it done.

The chase is cut after the walls are poured and the roof is on, the same as an electrical chase. A chainsaw, a reciprocating saw or a hot knife will all cut it, but Nudura names the hot knife as the best tool for water lines because it melts a clean channel exactly the size of the pipe.

The depth is the part that matters. The chase goes only as deep as the pipe needs, so the pipe sits tight against the back of the drywall with as much foam as possible left behind it, between the water and the concrete. A chase cut deeper than it has to be puts the pipe closer to the cold side of the wall, and that is how a supply line freezes in a wall that is otherwise R-22 or better. Follow the plumbing code on clearances, and stay as shallow as it allows.

Two more rules. Keep the pipe away from the fastening strips, which sit every 8 inches, because the drywall screws go into those strips and a screw does not care what is behind it. And if the plumber is sweating copper anywhere near the foam, the foam gets a sheet-metal shield first. EPS and an open flame do not mix.

Hose Bibs and the Freeze Line

A hose bib is the one fixture that has to live in an exterior wall, and it is the one most likely to split. In a climate that drops below freezing, Nudura's answer is a frost-free hose bib, the kind with its valve seat inside the warm part of the wall. If one is not available, a shut-off valve with a drain inside the house lets you empty the line before winter.

The sleeve for it goes in before the pour like any other, long enough to stand proud of the foam on both sides. That gives the plumber a coupler on each face and no foam to carve away.

Vent Stacks: Three Ways to Put One in an ICF Wall

Here is the number that surprises people about plumbing in ICF walls. The foam panel is 2 5/8 inches thick at its face, but the inside of it is moulded with dovetail channels that grip the concrete, and at its thinnest it is only 2 1/4 inches. So the largest pipe that fits wholly in the foam, couplings included, is 1 1/2 inches. A 3- or 4-inch stack will not fit in the foam alone.

When a stack has to run up an exterior wall, there are three ways to do it:

  1. Not recessed at all. The stack runs up the face of the wall and gets boxed in with a chase.
  2. Partly recessed. The foam is cut away after the pour and the pipe sits half in it, half out.
  3. Fully recessed. The pipe sits flush, partly into the concrete core.

The first two need no planning before the pour, and that is their appeal. The cost shows up later, as a bulkhead or a bump-out in the finished room that the owner will look at for as long as they own the house.

The third leaves nothing showing, but it has to be decided before the concrete arrives. A foam insert cut to the size of the stack is foamed into the form at the stack location and marked. The concrete flows around it, so the pocket is there when the plumber comes to cut the insert out. The stack can be no more than a third of the core depth. In a wall with a 6-inch core, that is a 2-inch pipe. And the concrete behind the pocket needs extra vertical reinforcing, because that stretch of wall is thinner than the rest. Once the plumber cuts the foam clear and sets the stack, it gets anchored in the pocket to code. The same insert method works for any drain or vent larger than 1 1/2 inches that has to run in the wall.

Getting a Pipe Past the Floor

There is one spot that catches people out. Where a floor system of joists or trusses hangs off the inside of the wall, a pipe running from one storey to the next may have to pass behind the rim. The plumber needs to reach that spot after the walls are poured and before the floor goes on.

That timing does not always line up. When it does not, Nudura's fix is simple: at each place a run will cross the floor line, the crew sets a length of PVC or ABS pipe vertically against the wall, long enough to stand above and below the floor. The floor is built around it, and later the plumber has an open path behind the joists instead of a sealed one.

Plumbing in ICF Walls: What to Settle Before the Pour

Most of the plumbing in a house never touches an exterior wall, and it can wait. This list cannot:

  • Where the water service comes in, and where the main drain goes out, with the drain's height and slope.
  • Every hose bib, and whether it is frost-free.
  • Any vent stack that has to run in an exterior wall, and whether it will be recessed.
  • Any supply line on an exterior wall, so the drywall layout can keep screws clear of it.
  • Where pipes cross the floor line behind the rim.
  • The other sleeves: tank vent, dryer, HRV, gas, and a spare or two.

The piping itself is your plumber's work, and so are the code and the inspection. What we control is the wall it passes through. Our own crews self-perform the footings, the ICF walls and the waterproofing, and we work alongside your builder and trades rather than replacing them. We are certified installers for Nudura, Amvic, Element and Fox, and we give architects and designers constructability input on ICF details before the drawings are finished. Send the plumbing layout with your quote request, even a sketch, so the sleeves and inserts go in where they belong the first time. The wiring follows much the same logic, covered in electrical in ICF walls, and what we form into a foundation is on our ICF foundations page.

FAQ

Can you run plumbing in ICF walls?

Yes, but the better plan is to avoid it. Supply lines and vents belong in interior partitions and floor spaces, the same as in a framed house. Where a pipe must run in an exterior wall, it goes in a chase cut into the inside foam after the pour, as shallow as the code allows.

How do pipes get through an ICF wall?

Through sleeves set in the forms before the concrete is placed, sized and located by the plumber. Each sleeve should extend past the foam on both sides so a coupler can go on later. A pipe missed before the pour means drilling through the concrete.

What size pipe fits in the foam of an ICF wall?

In a Nudura wall, 1 1/2 inches including couplings, because the foam is only 2 1/4 inches thick at its thinnest. Anything larger needs a foam insert set in the forms before the pour, so the pipe can sit partly in the concrete core.

Can a vent stack go in an ICF wall?

Yes, three ways: boxed in on the face of the wall, partly recessed into the foam, or fully recessed into the core. A fully recessed stack has to be planned before the pour, can be no more than a third of the core depth, and needs extra reinforcing behind it.

Will pipes freeze in an ICF wall?

Not if they are kept to interior walls where possible, and run shallow when they cannot be. A supply line in an exterior wall should sit tight to the back of the drywall with as much foam as possible behind it. Hose bibs should be frost-free.
