How Much Does a Home Addition Cost

The Square Footage Is Only Half the Story.

An addition is priced at the ground and at the joint with the old house. That is why two quotes on the same room can look nothing alike.

A do it yourself home addition is more doable than most people think, and harder in a different place than most people expect. The walls, the finishes and the trades are the parts people worry about. The trouble usually starts lower down, against a foundation you did not build and cannot see.

A new house starts on open ground. An addition starts against an old one. Every decision below grade has to agree with a footing somebody else poured, at a depth nobody wrote down, under a wall that has been settling quietly for decades. That is the part worth understanding before you pick up a shovel.

How much does a home addition cost? Anyone who answers that with a price per square foot, before they have seen your house, is quoting a house they have already built. The floor area is the part of an addition everyone measures, and it is only about half of what sets the price.

The other half sits where nobody measures. It is in the ground under the new room, in the joint where it meets the old house, and in the line each quote draws around what is included. Those are the parts that make two quotes on the same room look nothing alike, and they are worth understanding before you settle on a budget.

Why a Price Per Square Foot Gets It Wrong

A per-square-foot number assumes cost grows with size. On an addition, a large share of it does not.

Every addition, from bumping out a kitchen to adding a full second wing, needs the same short list before a single wall goes up: drawings, an engineered design for how the new structure connects to the old one, a permit with its inspections, a machine on site to dig, and a concrete truck to pour the footings. A small bump-out needs almost all of that. So does a large wing.

Spread that list over a few hundred square feet and the per-foot figure looks alarming. Spread it over a whole wing and it looks reasonable. Same list, same work, different arithmetic.

That is why the smallest additions often look the most expensive per foot, and why an average pulled from a website tells you so little about yours. It describes an average house, on an average lot, joined to an average wall. Nobody owns one of those.

How Much Does a Home Addition Cost Below Grade?

Any structural addition needs footings and a foundation of its own. That is where the widest swings in price live, and none of it shows once the room is finished.

On our foundation work, the site moves the number more than any other single factor. A flat lot with clean access is a different job from a sloped one with rock at two feet. Stepped footings, scribing forms to bedrock and getting machines in and out all cost labour, and labour is where foundation prices part ways.

Additions make access harder than a new house does. The new footprint usually goes in the tightest part of the lot: between the house and a lot line, where a deck stands, beside a driveway, over the spot where the services come in. An excavator and a concrete pump that could drive straight onto a vacant lot may have to work around landscaping, a septic bed or a neighbour's fence. Each of those is time, and on a foundation, time is the number.

Then there is the footing that is already there. Dig beside it and you remove the soil holding it in place. Dig below it and you can undermine it. How deep the new footing goes, and whether the old one needs support while the work happens, is an engineer's call. It is a line on the price whether you see it coming or not. We go through what goes wrong down there in our post on the owner-built addition.

The Joint With the Old House

An addition has to do two hard things at once: respect the house that is already there, and give you space that feels like it was always part of the plan. The second is design. The first is a cost a new house never carries.

The connection details are engineered case by case, because no two existing houses are built the same way. The new floor has to land at the old floor's height. The new roof has to meet the old one without trapping water or snow. And the wall you are opening into has to be opened, which means finding out what is inside it: wiring, plumbing, a duct, a beam nobody drew.

That is why an addition with us starts with an on-site assessment of the existing structure and the grades, before anybody prices the new room. The old house decides more of the cost than people expect. How the connection is actually built is in our post on how an ICF addition ties into an existing house.

Living in the house through the work has a cost too. Most of the structural work on an addition happens outside the existing exterior wall, and the breakthrough can be sequenced for late in the schedule, so the household carries on. Keeping the occupied home protected from the weather while it is open is part of the job. It belongs in the price, not in an argument halfway through.

Two Quotes on the Same Room

When two prices for the same addition are far apart, the usual reason is not that one builder is greedy. It is that the two quotes drew the line in different places.

Footings, foundation walls, waterproofing, drainage, the connection to the old house, weather protection while the wall is open: each of those can be in a quote or left out of it. Two quotes drawing that line differently is the most common reason two numbers look far apart when the work is nearly identical. The cheaper one is often the one that left the hardest part for you to discover later.

So the honest reply to "how much does a home addition cost" starts with a question back: what is in the quote? Read a quote for what it includes before you read its total. On an addition with us, the scope covers:

  • an on-site assessment of the existing structure and grades
  • footings and foundation for the new footprint
  • ICF walls tied into the existing structure
  • coordination with your designer or engineer on the connection details
  • weather protection for the occupied home during construction

We itemize it, so you can see which side of the line each item sits on. The same honesty runs the other way. Some things get oversold below grade: a second drainage board, waterproofing on a frost wall, a membrane under the basement slab. We would rather take a line off your quote than sell you a detail the assembly does not need.

The Wall: What It Costs Now and What It Costs Later

The wall you choose changes the price twice. Once on the quote, and once every winter after.

An insulated concrete wall usually costs more to put up than a bare framed one. The fair comparison is not the wall alone, though. An ICF wall arrives with the structure, the insulation on both faces and a fastening surface already in it, so there is no interior framing, no batt, no vapour barrier and no strapping to add afterwards. Those are trades and materials that come off the rest of the build. We go through where that premium actually sits in what ICF actually costs.

The later cost is the one people forget to price. An addition has more outside wall and roof for its size than the house it joins, plus a seam where old meets new, so it loses heat faster than the rooms it sits beside. That is why additions are notorious for being the coldest room in the house. A continuous R-22+ ICF wall ends that before it starts. A cold room is not a finishing problem you fix later. It is a cost you pay every month for as long as you own the house.

How Much Does a Home Addition Cost on Your Lot?

The only honest number is one built from your drawings and your site. We quote every job off both, and the consultation and the quote are free, with no obligation.

The process is short. You send what you have, and we get back to you to book a consultation: usually a call first, then a site walk if the project is a fit. What helps most:

  • the property address
  • drawings or sketches, even rough ones
  • photos of the site, including the side of the house the addition will sit against, and especially any exposed rock
  • a rough sense of timing

Not having drawings yet is no reason to wait. We can work with you and your designer from the earliest stage, and that is often where an addition saves money: a site reality caught before the drawings are finished costs a pencil line, not a change order. You can see the full scope on our additions page, or send us what you have.

FAQ

Why do small additions cost more per square foot?

Because the fixed items barely shrink. Drawings, engineering for the connection, the permit and its inspections, and getting a machine and concrete to the site are needed for a small addition almost as much as for a large one. Spread over fewer square feet, they push the per-foot figure up.

Is it cheaper to build up or out?

Neither, by default. Building out needs new footings and a foundation, which is where site conditions show up in the price. Building up skips the new foundation but asks the existing one, and the walls on it, to carry another storey, and an engineer has to confirm they can before anyone prices it. The house you have decides which is cheaper.

Is a quote for an addition free?

Yes. The consultation and the quote are free, with no obligation, and the quote is itemized so you can see exactly what is in it and what is not.

Do I need drawings before I can get a price?

No. We can start from a sketch and photos of the site, and work with you and your designer from the earliest stage. We quote off the drawings and the site, so the number firms up as the drawings do, and flagging the site's realities before they are finished often saves money.
