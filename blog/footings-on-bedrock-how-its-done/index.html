Footings on Bedrock: How It's Actually Done

When the excavator hits granite two feet down, the standard playbook stops working. Here's how footings get scribed, pinned and poured on the Canadian Shield.

  1. Home
  2. Blog
  3. Footings on Bedrock: How It's Actually Done

Sooner or later, everyone who builds in cottage country meets the Canadian Shield. The topsoil runs out, the bucket rings off granite, and the standard footing detail — level trench, level forms, level pour — becomes useless. What happens next separates crews that build on rock from crews that fight it.

Step 1: Read the rock

Sound, exposed bedrock is the best bearing surface in construction — there is nothing beneath it to settle. But it has to be sound: the surface gets cleaned and assessed, loose and fractured material comes off, and the layout is set against the rock that's actually there, not the rock the drawings hoped for.

Step 2: Scribe the forms

This is the craft. Because the rock rises and falls, every form board is scribed — cut to follow the stone's exact profile — so the bottom of the form meets the rock tight along its full length. Done right, the footing bears fully on granite everywhere, with no voids underneath and no concrete bleeding out under the forms. Done wrong, you get point loads and honeycombing. It is slow, precise, boat-builder kind of work, and it's the heart of the job.

Step 3: Pin it to the Shield

Footings on rock don't just sit — they're anchored. Holes are drilled into the sound rock, cleaned, and rebar dowels are set in structural epoxy. Those dowels tie the footing to the bedrock and carry up into the reinforcement above, so footing, foundation and structure become one continuous load path into the planet itself.

Step 4: Step the pour

The footing steps with the rock — level runs connected by vertical steps — engineered so every run bears on stone and every top lands where the foundation needs it. On a steep site the stepped ICF foundation then climbs from the footings to a level main floor.

Why it's worth it

A footing scribed and pinned to bedrock will never settle, never heave, never move. For lakefront lots on the Shield it isn't just the right way to build — it's frequently the only honest one. It's also, not coincidentally, DMT's specialty.
