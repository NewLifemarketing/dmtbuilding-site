How Long Does It Take to Build a Cottage

Count It in Seasons, Not Weeks

The crew is rarely what sets the date. The paperwork, the lot, the road in and the time of year do.

How long does it take to build a cottage? The honest unit is seasons, not weeks. The crew is rarely what decides it. Drawings, permits, the ground, the road in and the time of year do, and on a cottage lot every one of those is harder than it is in town.

That is why two cottages of the same size can finish a year apart. One sits on a flat lot off a paved road with its approvals in hand by spring. The other sits on rock by the water, reached by barge, with a shoreline permit still in review. The building is the same. The calendar is not.

How Long Does It Take to Build a Cottage? Longer Before the Dig

People picture the timeline starting with an excavator. It starts months earlier, at a kitchen table, and that first stretch is usually the longest and the least visible.

There are three pieces of paperwork, and each one waits on the one before it:

  • Drawings. A designer draws it, an engineer confirms the structure, and the set goes back and forth until it matches the lot. Plans drawn for a flat lot rarely survive contact with a sloped one.
  • Shoreline approvals. Building near water, a wetland or a steep slope can need the conservation authority's permission as well as the municipality's. That review runs on its own clock, and it can ask for changes that send the drawings back a step.
  • The building permit. The building department has a set period to decide a complete application for a house, 10 days under the building code. The word that matters is complete. The clock does not start until everything it asks for is in.

None of that is wasted time. It is the cheapest place in the whole project to change your mind. We cannot shorten it from the job site, either. Before we mobilize we need two things from you: drawings and permits. Our post on building a cottage on your property covers the questions to ask the township before any of this starts.

The way to take weeks out of this stage is to bring the builder in early. We can work with you and your designer before the drawings are finished, flagging rock, grade and access while they are still lines on paper. A problem found there costs an eraser. Found on site, it costs a season.

The Lot and the Season Set the Pace

Once the permits are in hand, the ground takes over. A cottage lot is rarely the level, sandy pad a standard house plan assumes, and the foundation is where that shows up first.

Rock, Slope and Shape

On a typical job we form roughly 100 linear feet of footing a day, plus pouring and then stripping the forms. That figure is for a straightforward rectangle. Corners, steps, curves and changes in height all cost labour, and a cottage on a slope toward the water is usually all four at once. Footings scribed onto bedrock take longer again, because the form has to follow the rock rather than a string line. The trade-off is worth it: sound rock has nothing under it to settle. How to build a cottage foundation goes through which foundation suits which lot.

What a slope adds is not only time on the forms. Every step in the footing is a step in the wall above it, and each one has to be set before the pour. Get the layout right and the walls go up fast. Get it wrong and nothing downstream recovers the time.

How Long Does It Take to Build a Cottage You Reach by Boat?

Here is the part of the question that has nothing to do with construction. Every block, bar of steel and cubic metre of concrete has to reach the lot, and a pour cannot stop halfway because the truck could not make the last hill.

A lot on a year-round road is the easy case. A seasonal road, a steep driveway or a water-access island changes the order of the whole job, because deliveries have to line up with the days the route is open. Concrete work runs on logistics: forms, steel, ready-mix, pumps and haulage arriving in the right order, on the right day. On a hard site we plan barge or winter access in the quote stage with our haulage and pumping partners. Difficult access is a solvable problem. It just has to be planned, not discovered.

On a water-access lot, the timeline is mostly about boats, ice and weather windows. The building itself takes about as long as it would on the mainland.

Winter Slows the Ground, Not the Concrete

Most people assume nothing gets built after freeze-up. Some things do stop. Excavating frozen ground is slow and expensive, and nobody should pour a footing onto frozen soil. If the hole is not dug before the ground locks up, the start often waits for spring.

Once the footings are in, the walls are a different story. Concrete needs to stay above 4°C for its first 24 hours to cure properly. In bare plywood forms that means blankets and heaters. In ICF the foam on both faces insulates the core from the moment it is poured, so the wall holds its own heat and gives it up slowly and evenly. We pour ICF walls in February; the work does not stop for the weather.

So the season that matters most is the one when you dig. A cottage that is excavated in the fall can have its foundation and walls up through the winter and be ready for the trades in spring. One that misses the fall dig usually loses the winter entirely. Our post on how long it takes to build an ICF house walks the below-grade stages and the inspections between them in more detail.

Those inspections belong in the plan, too. The building code has the permit holder notify the building department before the footings go in and again before the foundation is backfilled. On a remote lot, a missed inspection is not a lost afternoon. It can be a lost week, because the inspector, the excavator and the crew all have to make the same drive again.

The Shell Is Quick. The Inside Is Not.

Ask anybody who has built one where the months went and few of them will say the walls. The walls are the fast part. ICF walls go up faster than block or a poured-and-framed wall because structure, insulation and the surface you fasten to all happen in one step.

That also takes work off the schedule. There is no interior stud wall to frame, no batt to fit, no vapour barrier to hang and no strapping. That is three trades and three material orders off the critical path, and the floor above can start sooner. On a remote lot that matters more, not less, because every trade that does not have to drive in is a week that does not depend on somebody else's calendar.

After the shell, the clock belongs to the people who finish it: the roof, windows, plumbing, electrical, the septic and well if the lot needs them, drywall, cabinets and floors. Each one has to book a visit, and on a cottage each visit is a drive. That is where most of the calendar goes, and it is the part a homeowner can influence most. Decide finishes early, order long-lead items like windows before the walls are up, and book trades for the dates the shell will be ready rather than the dates you hope it will be.

One more decision changes the length of the whole job: whether the cottage is heated in winter. A three-season building can skip work a four-season one cannot. Building three-season now and four-season later usually means doing that work twice. Decide it before the drawings are sealed, because the answer changes the foundation, the insulation, the plumbing and the mechanical system, and every one of those is cheaper to draw than to redo.

What Your Lot Will Do to the Calendar

Nobody can answer "how long does it take to build a cottage?" honestly without seeing the lot it sits on. What we can do is give you a realistic timeline in the quote, built from your drawings, your site and the season you want to start. Overall schedule depends on design, permits and site conditions, and on a cottage the site is usually the biggest of the three.

Before you ask any builder for a date, have these ready:

  1. The property address, so the access and the approvals can be checked.
  2. Drawings or sketches, however rough. A sketch is enough to start the conversation.
  3. Photos of the site, especially any exposed rock, the slope toward the water and the road in.
  4. A rough sense of timing, including whether you want to use it next summer or are building for the long term.

The more of that we have, the faster and sharper the quote. We build the concrete scope with our own crew from start to finish and work alongside your builder and trades for everything after it. If you want to know what your lot will do to your calendar, send us what you have and we will walk it with you.

FAQ

Can a cottage be built in one summer?

The construction sometimes can, if the drawings and permits are finished before spring and the lot is easy to reach. The approvals usually cannot be squeezed into the same summer, so a one-summer build is really a one-summer build after a winter of paperwork.

Does a water-access lot add time to the build?

Not to the building itself, which takes about as long as it would on the mainland. What changes is the planning: every delivery and pour has to line up with barge days or safe ice, so the schedule is built around access first and the construction second.

Is ICF slower to build than wood frame?

No. ICF is faster at the wall stage, because structure, insulation and the fastening surface go up in one step, and it takes interior framing, batt insulation, vapour barrier and strapping off the schedule entirely.

Can you build a cottage foundation in winter?

Footings need unfrozen ground, so the excavation and footings are best done before freeze-up. Once they are in, ICF walls can be poured through the winter, because the foam keeps the curing concrete above the temperature it needs.
